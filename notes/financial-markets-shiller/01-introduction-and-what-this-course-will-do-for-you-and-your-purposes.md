# Financial Markets

## Chapter 1: Introduction and What This Course Will Do for You and Your Purposes

Economics 252 is titled, simply, Financial Markets, and Robert Shiller means the title literally. By putting "markets" rather than "theory" or "economics" in front of students, he is signaling that the course is down to earth — about the real world, about what people do with their lives, about society itself. It could sound like a course about trading, but Shiller frames it more broadly than that. Finance, as he puts it in the course description, is a pillar of civilized society: the structure through which resources are allocated across space and time, the mechanism that incentivizes people toward productive activity, the machinery that sponsors ventures bringing many people together while making sure they are treated fairly and rewarded for what they contribute. And because anything ambitious that people undertake carries uncertainty, finance is also, fundamentally, about managing risk. The course carries a philosophical underpinning, but Shiller is emphatic that it will stay focused on detail — on the particular institutions of banking, insurance, securities, futures markets, and derivatives markets, and on the financial crises that punctuate their history. It is also, he insists, a course about the future, however hard the future is to predict.

The course has a U.S. bias, since Shiller knows the United States best, but he is conscious that most of his students will end up working outside it, and doubly conscious that Economics 252 has an audience well beyond the room he is lecturing in. It is one of roughly two dozen Yale courses distributed free to the world through Open Yale, filmed by a single cameraman, Dan Cody, and eventually posted online. This is the second time the course has been filmed for Open Yale — the first was in 2008 — and Shiller argues that finance, more than almost any other subject on the platform, needed updating. A physics course ages by three years of new research; finance had just been through the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression, a worldwide event that governments were actively rewriting institutions to respond to, with the G-20 treating financial reform as a top item on its agenda for international cooperation. Keeping the course current, and keeping it international in outlook, is part of why this second filming exists.

Yale offers a second major finance course, Economics 251, "Financial Theory," taught by John Geanakoplos, a mathematical economist who is also research director for Ellington Capital. Where Shiller and Geanakoplos overlap is in bridging theory and practice; where they diverge is emphasis. Geanakoplos's syllabus runs through topics like utilities, endowments, and equilibrium; the mathematical theory of bond pricing; dynamic present value; Social Security and the overlapping-generations model; uncertainty and hedging; state pricing; the theory of risk and the Capital Asset Pricing Model; and the leverage cycle. Shiller recommends it but does not require it — Economics 252 is self-contained, and he intends to keep mathematics to a minimum without eliminating it. Because most students will not take Econ 251, six review sessions run by the teaching assistants cover the underlying theory in condensed form, paired with six problem sets, so that some real mathematical content still runs through the course even outside the lectures themselves.

### Purpose, Not Vocation

Shiller has taught this course since the fall of 1985 — twenty-five years by the time of this filming — and describes a recurring source of pride: giving talks on Wall Street and asking for a show of hands from former students, and always finding one or two in the room. He is careful, though, to distinguish the course's usefulness from a vocational purpose. Yale has a long tradition of not being a vocational school; the 1701 charter from the Connecticut colonial government describes the college as founded "for the educating and instructing of youth in good literature, arts, and sciences," and Shiller locates his course within that tradition rather than against it — vocational in a sense, but not, in his words, "vulgar vocational." He wants students to think about how the material fits into their lives, not merely how to get a job from it. His preferred image is that finance is a kind of engineering — one that works not with a technical apparatus but with people, which means the technical apparatus still has to be mastered before the engineering can be done well.

The assigned textbook, chosen for this purpose, is by Frank Fabozzi with two co-authors: Franco Modigliani, who was Shiller's dissertation adviser at MIT and died in 2003, and Frank Jones of Guardian Life Insurance Company. Shiller has co-authored research papers with both Fabozzi and Modigliani, and describes discovering how much he still didn't know about the subject when he first read the book — poolside, on vacation with Jeremy Siegel of the Wharton School, an odd choice of reading material next to novels but one he found genuinely absorbing. The comparison he draws is to learning a language: finance has thousands of specific terms and mechanisms, and exams in the course will test the details — how municipal securities are classified, how rating agencies rate them — not broad generalities. Alongside the textbook, Shiller is drafting his own book, provisionally titled *Finance and the Good Society* (a title he holds loosely, since another author could claim it first), and making draft chapters available to the class through the course site. He treats the exercise of writing a book while teaching the same material as a way of keeping the writing alive rather than sterile, open to being shaped by student reaction as it develops.

### Wealth, Purpose, and the Gospel of Wealth

*Finance and the Good Society* is meant to answer an objection Shiller takes seriously: that the phrase sounds like an oxymoron, because people are angry at finance. The anger is understandable — the worst crisis since the 1930s, a sense that some people got rich at others' expense or lobbied governments into bailouts — but Shiller does not think finance as a whole is to blame, and he thinks of it instead as a noble profession that the developing world is actively adopting as a driver of growth. To make the case, he turns to data on who actually gets rich from it. Working from Bureau of Labor Statistics figures on U.S. occupations in 2008 with projections to 2018, he shows financial analysts numbering close to 300,000, financial managers over half a million, and personal financial advisers around a quarter million — dwarfing economists (roughly 20,000, and that number likely excludes professors), astronomers, sociologists, political scientists, and mathematicians. He adds, deliberately, one further comparison: massage therapists outnumber all of those academic fields combined by something like a hundred to one, a reminder of the mismatch between specialized university training and where the job market actually sits.

The same disproportion shows up at the very top. Shiller reports having gone through Forbes's annual list of the 400 richest Americans and finding, to his own surprise, not a single professional athlete or movie star on it. Oprah Winfrey appears, but as a businessperson who builds and runs organizations, not simply as an entertainer; Steven Spielberg appears too, through DreamWorks, the company he built to finance films rather than through directing alone. Bill Gates is the closest thing to a bestselling author on the list, for *The Road Ahead*. About a third of the people on the Forbes 400 inherited their fortunes; most made them, and what they mostly did was build organizations at scale — raising capital, doing deals, absorbing other companies — in businesses that are frequently, in Shiller's word, boring. Most of the people on the list are unknown to the public; a separate Forbes Celebrity 100 exists, and the overlap between the two lists is almost nonexistent (Oprah again, and Spielberg).

That observation leads to the harder question: what does a person do with a fortune once the Forbes 400 threshold — a minimum of a billion dollars — is cleared? Twenty houses and any number of sports cars still leave nine hundred million dollars unspent, and Shiller notes a long historical suspicion of people who try to solve that problem by maximizing the visible display of their wealth. Sumptuary laws — restrictions on conspicuous consumption — appear across many countries and periods going back at least to the Locrian code in ancient Greece around 700 BC, which Shiller reads as evidence that treating the accumulation of wealth as an end in itself has never sat comfortably with most societies.

The alternative answer, in the reading assigned for this opening week, comes from Andrew Carnegie. Carnegie made his fortune through the Carnegie Steel Company and wrote a short 1889 magazine article titled "Wealth," in a periodical of that name, whose closing paragraph coined the phrase "gospel of wealth" — a phrase picked up around the world, mostly as a provocation, and later used as the title of Carnegie's book *The Gospel of Wealth and Other Essays*. Carnegie's argument was that some people are simply better than others at what he called "affairs" — business — and that this talent carries a moral obligation: once such a person reaches middle age, having made a great deal of money, that fortune must be given away productively before death, because passing it to children who most likely lack the same drive amounts to squandering it on conspicuous consumption. Carnegie practiced what he preached at a scale that still marks the American landscape: the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University), the Carnegie Endowment for World Peace, Carnegie Hall in New York, and, in a smaller but telling instance, Lake Carnegie at Princeton, created because Carnegie, visiting and noticing swampy ground, offered to fund a lake — along with the prize for the first rowing competition held on it. In 1914, Thomas Edison, developing sound film and wanting his first sound picture to feature "geniuses of our time," recorded Carnegie reading from *The Gospel of Wealth*; the visual reel was lost, but the soundtrack survives as the only recording of Carnegie's voice. A California newspaper's 1890 review of the original essay, which Shiller also assigns, took the opposite view sharply: that people who make fortunes are not obviously the most enlightened members of society, and that calling wealth accumulation a noble cause was absurd on its face. Shiller places himself between the two positions, and connects Carnegie's argument to a modern echo — the campaign led by Bill Gates and Warren Buffett to persuade billionaires to commit most of their wealth to philanthropy while still alive. He flags, without resolving it, the broader trend of rising concentration of wealth at the top, and a separate worry that a field so visibly rewarding right now may simply be experiencing a bubble of its own — swamped by newcomers until the rewards inevitably thin out, even as he expects that the wealthiest people in the world will, for structural reasons tied to the power of financial technology, keep being finance-related people with an accompanying obligation to give productively.

### Guest Speakers and Teaching Staff

To make that moral argument concrete rather than abstract, Shiller brings in outside speakers who are, in his description, rich but not selfish. David Swensen, Yale's Chief Investment Officer since 1985 — the same year Shiller began teaching this course — took an endowment of under a billion dollars and grew it to $22.9 billion by 2008; the financial crisis brought it down, but it still stood at $16.7 billion as of June 2010. Swensen also teaches Economics 450 with Dean Takahashi, and Shiller frames him as proof that a genuinely gifted money manager can beat the market while repeatedly turning down far larger Wall Street compensation to stay at Yale. Maurice "Hank" Greenberg, the second speaker, took charge of the North American operations of what became AIG in 1962, under C.V. Starr, when the company was failing, and built it over decades as CEO into the largest insurance company in the world before leaving in 2005 — three years before AIG required the largest government bailout in the crisis, in a business unit Shiller is careful to note Greenberg had no responsibility for by that point. Greenberg is Vice Chairman of the Council on Foreign Relations and funded the Greenberg Center at Yale, next to the Center for Globalization. The third speaker, Laura Cha, will appear by video link from Hong Kong: a non-official member of Hong Kong's Executive Council, the first non-Chinese delegate to the National People's Congress representing Hong Kong, and a former vice chair of the China Securities Regulatory Commission, giving the course a regulator's perspective that Shiller values as a counterweight to the stories of wealth accumulation.

The teaching staff carries its own thread through the course. Oliver Bunn, from Germany and the University of Bonn, is the head teaching assistant coordinating the section. Elan Fuld is studying the economics of the pizza delivery industry as an applied case of real-world economic theory. Bige Kahraman, from Bilkent University in Turkey, works in **behavioral finance** — the application of psychology, sociology, and the other social sciences to financial questions, a field Shiller describes as having transformed finance over the last twenty years from what academia treated as an almost purely mathematical discipline. Her dissertation examines why mutual funds offer complicated, differentiated fee schedules — not unlike a confusing cell phone plan — and finds evidence that clients are sometimes steered toward fee structures that serve the fund company's competitive position more than the client's interest, a finding Shiller connects directly to why financial regulators like Laura Cha matter. Bin Li, from Beijing and educated at University College London, works on leveraged asset pricing and behavioral finance as well.

### The Twenty Lectures Ahead

Shiller closes the opening lecture by walking through the shape of the rest of the course. Lecture 2 covers risk and the financial crisis: independent risks can be diversified away under the law of large numbers, but the 2008 crisis exposed how much less independent real-world risks are than portfolio theory assumed, a failure of the independence assumption that Shiller treats as central to why the crisis happened. He draws the parallel to 1929–1933, when the stock market crash spiraled into a Depression that left 25% of the U.S. workforce unemployed by 1933 and was only resolved, grimly, by the stimulus of drafting the unemployed into the Second World War — and contrasts that with the controversial but faster bailout response engineered this time by central bankers such as Ben Bernanke and Mervyn King. Lecture 3 treats finance as a technology in the engineering sense, as complex in its instruments as an airplane or a nuclear reactor, drawing on Shiller's own 2003 book *The New Financial Order* for a look forward. Lecture 4 covers portfolio diversification and a brief treatment of the Capital Asset Pricing Model, the same theory Geanakoplos covers with more rigor in Econ 251. Lecture 5 is on insurance, tracing the industry back to intuitive risk-pooling in Ancient Rome but dating its real development to the 1600s invention of probability theory; Shiller uses the contrast between the 2010 Haiti earthquake and an earlier San Francisco earthquake of similar magnitude — vastly higher loss of life and property in Haiti — to argue that a functioning insurance industry, which also polices building codes as a condition of coverage, does more protective work at scale than charity ever can.

Lecture 6 takes on the Efficient Markets Theory, the view developed in the 1960s that competition prices securities so well that beating the market is essentially hopeless; Shiller signals his own skepticism while granting it an element of truth, and points to Swensen as a working counterexample. Lecture 7 covers debt markets and the Federal Reserve's role in coordinating them through open market operations and quantitative easing, alongside the everyday case for borrowing, such as a mortgage, in economies where such markets exist. Lecture 8 covers the stock market, framed not as a casino — despite Karl Marx's characterization — but as an institutional solution to the problem of getting people with different amounts of capital and different amounts of time and effort to combine productively. Lecture 9 covers real estate, an area of long personal interest for Shiller and the subject of the S&P/Case-Shiller Home Price Indices he built with Karl Case; he attributes the crisis substantially to a psychologically driven bubble in home prices that collapsed around 2006 and notes similar dynamics re-emerging elsewhere in the world. Lecture 10 covers behavioral finance in full.

The lecture numbering then jumps: Lecture 12 covers banking, the money multiplier, and bank regulation, including Basel III, the new international accord out of Basel, Switzerland, endorsed by the G-20 at its Seoul meeting. Lecture 13 covers forward and futures markets, including the over-the-counter, bank-intermediated forward contracts and the exchange-traded futures contracts on venues like the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, where Shiller has been directly involved in building a futures market for single-family homes based on the Case-Shiller Index. Lecture 14 covers options markets and the added complexity options introduce into financial theory. Lecture 15 covers monetary policy and the role central banks like the Federal Reserve played in containing the recent crisis. Lecture 16 covers investment banking — capital-raising, security issuance and retirement, and firms like Goldman Sachs — alongside the new regulatory landscape created by the Dodd-Frank Act, passed that July in the United States, and comparable reforms from the European Parliament. Lecture 17 covers professional money managers and the argument that institutional investors, once they hold a large enough stake to be heard at a shareholders' meeting, become stewards of a genuine public interest rather than simply profit-seekers. Lecture 18 covers exchanges, brokers, dealers, and clearinghouses, and the proliferation of stock exchanges worldwide alongside the rise of electronic and microsecond computer-to-computer trading. Lecture 19 covers public and nonprofit finance — how cities like New Haven finance infrastructure through debt issuance, and how nonprofit organizations, structured without shareholders, can be built by anyone willing to learn the mechanics. The course closes with Lecture 20, "Finding Your Purpose in Finance," which Shiller previews as a deliberate return to the course's opening theme: that the point of financial success, referencing Robert Frank's *Richistan* on the habits of the newly wealthy, is not personal consumption but the moral purpose a person chooses to pursue with it.

## Glossary (this chapter)
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Behavioral Finance | The application of psychology, sociology, and other social sciences to financial questions — a field Shiller describes as having transformed academic finance over the last twenty years away from a purely mathematical discipline. |
| Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) | A mathematical theory of portfolio diversification, covered briefly in this course and with more rigor in Economics 251. |
| Efficient Markets Theory | A theory developed in the 1960s holding that competitive financial markets push prices to a level that already incorporates all available information, making it effectively impossible to consistently beat the market through trading. |
| Law of Large Numbers | The principle that a portfolio containing a large number of independent risks will see those risks average out, eliminating most of the risk — provided the risks are genuinely independent. |
| Sumptuary laws | Laws, found across many countries and periods (dating at least to the Locrian code in Ancient Greece, c. 700 BC), that prohibit conspicuous consumption or the ostentatious display of wealth. |
| Gospel of Wealth | Andrew Carnegie's 1889 doctrine, later expanded into a book, that individuals gifted at business have a moral obligation to give away their fortunes productively during their lifetimes rather than pass them to heirs. |
| Forbes 400 | Forbes Magazine's annual list of the 400 richest people in America; as discussed in this chapter, the minimum threshold to appear on it is roughly one billion dollars in net worth. |
| Quantitative Easing | A tool used by central banks, alongside traditional open market operations, to coordinate and influence debt markets. |
| Basel III | An international bank regulation accord, negotiated in Basel, Switzerland, and endorsed by the G-20 at its Seoul meeting, developed in response to the financial crisis. |
| Dodd-Frank Act | U.S. legislation passed in July 2010 that changed the regulatory structure governing investment banks and other financial institutions. |
| Open Yale | Yale University's program offering roughly two dozen full course recordings free to the public; this course's first Open Yale filming was in 2008, with this lecture beginning its second. |
| Revenue bonds | Debt instruments issued by municipalities, such as the City of New Haven, to finance public projects like roads, schools, and infrastructure. |
| S&P/Case-Shiller Home Price Indices | Home price indices developed by Robert Shiller and Karl Case, referenced here as central to Shiller's work on real estate and later to a related futures market at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange. |
